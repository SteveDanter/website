MG Metro Turbo

I loved this car.

My Metro Turbo was black with red decals. It also possessed all the luxuries a young driver could possibly desire: seat belts, carpets and a set of wonderfully tacky covers that I fitted to the windscreen-wiper arms.

Classy.

After the Capri’s increasingly questionable steering, the Metro felt as though it could go around anything. In my youthful imagination, you could drive it at 100 mph everywhere and never needed to slow down for corners—it simply went around them.

British lightweight engineering

The longer you owned a Metro, the faster it became. I think this was mainly due to the continual weight reduction caused by erosion around the wheel arches, sills, door panels and just about every other piece of metal attached to the car.

Rust was simply British Leyland’s version of lightweight engineering.

At some point, I replaced both front wings with pattern parts bought from the local car-parts dealer. They may not have been genuine MG panels, but they contained considerably more metal than the ones they replaced, which seemed like an improvement.

The binary clutch

My left leg eventually became twice the size of my right because the clutch pedal was so heavy.

Clutch control was also something of an optimistic description. The clutch was either fully engaged or completely disengaged; there was no meaningful state between the two.

Every journey began with a small gamble: a clean departure, a stalled engine or an accidental racing start.

Where does the Suzuki fit?

The precise order of some of these vehicles remains a little hazy. Somewhere during this period, I also owned my Suzuki GS550L motorcycle.

As far as I can remember, Karen travelled with me on the Suzuki when we went to visit Vince. I can picture her on the bike, but I can’t clearly remember her travelling in the Metro.

That doesn’t necessarily mean she didn’t—it merely demonstrates that motorcycles have apparently occupied the more reliable part of my memory.

Gwyn’s Hydragas pump

The Metro had Hydragas suspension. One year, I was due to drive to Leyland for New Year’s Eve, but before setting off I decided to replace the bearings in one of the rear suspension arms.

Replacing the bearings required me to depressurise the Hydragas system. Releasing the pressure was easy enough, and the bearings were successfully changed.

Unfortunately, I was then left with a Metro sitting on its deflated suspension—and every garage capable of pumping it back up was closed for Christmas and New Year.

Fortunately, I was carrying out the work in Gwyn’s farm workshop.

Gwyn made a suspension pump from an old car bottle jack. He effectively reversed its action so that, instead of the hydraulic pressure lifting the jack, it forced the fluid back out through the filler hole. A length of tubing then carried the fluid into the filler nipple on the Metro’s suspension.

It was pure genius.

The homemade contraption restored the suspension well enough for me to make the journey to Leyland. When I had the pressure checked properly after returning home, it was spot-on. It didn’t require so much as a top-up.

If Gwyn needed it, Gwyn built it

Gwyn is something of a mechanical genius. If he wants something and doesn’t have it, he simply builds it.

He once developed a pump to move water from a stream at the bottom of his farm up to a reservoir near the house. It worked by collecting water on a pivoting tray. Each time the tray became full, it tipped and emptied itself before returning to its original position to fill again. That continual movement powered the pump that carried the water uphill.

No electricity, no engine—just flowing water, gravity and Gwyn refusing to accept that something couldn’t be done.

He also built his own drive-on car jack so that he could work underneath vehicles more easily, along with far too many other ingenious creations to mention.

Some people buy the tool required for a job. Gwyn starts with an old bottle jack, a few pieces of scrap metal and a firm belief that the laws of physics will eventually cooperate.

Back to two wheels

Eventually, I part-exchanged the Metro for the mighty Kawasaki GPZ1000RX.

My old Army friend Vince—who would later become my best man—had suggested that we spend two weeks touring southern Europe on our motorcycles. There was just one problem: I didn’t own a suitable motorcycle at the time.

The Metro therefore went to South Wales Superbikes, and I rode away on the Kawasaki.

One turbocharged adventure had ended. Another, much faster one was about to begin.
