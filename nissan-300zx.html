Nissan 300ZX

I got this one for free from my good friend and ex-cousin-by-marriage, Simon.

It had been sitting on his driveway for a good few years and had definitely seen better days, but I thought it might make a good project for Liam and me. Liam would only have been about twelve at the time, so this seemed like an excellent opportunity for some father-and-son bonding involving an old Japanese sports car and, as it turned out, rather more rust than I had anticipated.

Getting it home

We went up to Simon's house armed with a tyre pump and a fully charged battery to see whether we could bring it back to life.

Typical Japanese car — despite having sat there for years, it fired up almost straight away. The tyres inflated too and, despite having stood for so long, they were in surprisingly good condition. They were only flat at the bottom.

So I did the sensible thing and tentatively drove it home. The brakes were shocking and, once we started moving, it became apparent that tyres which are only flat at the bottom don't immediately become round again. Combined with the worn-out shock absorbers, it bounced along the road like a kangaroo. Still, we made it home.

Three pages of failure

I ordered various second-hand parts online and gradually got the car into a condition where I thought it was worth presenting it for an MOT.

The MOT tester disagreed.

The failure list ran to three pages.

Not to be defeated, Gwyn welded the chassis, I bodged up the exhaust and repaired everything else on the list that needed attention. Eventually I drove it back to the MOT station for the retest.

It passed.

We now had a road-legal Nissan 300ZX that had cost almost nothing to buy, mainly because it had cost exactly nothing to buy.

Just like the Ferraris

Once it was back on the road, Liam and I drove it around quite a bit and even took it across to Guernsey on the ferry.

Liam had a particularly good reason for wanting to take the 300ZX.

On previous ferry trips we'd seen Ferraris, Porsches and other low sports cars being carefully guided on and off the ship by the loading crew. Because of their limited ground clearance, crew members would stand around watching the ramps and directing the drivers to make sure they didn't scrape the underside of their expensive cars.

According to the approximately twelve-year-old Liam, our 300ZX was also very low. Therefore, when we arrived at the ferry, the loading crew would obviously have to give us the same special treatment.

Better still, all the other passengers would be watching and thinking how incredibly sporty our car must be if it required this much attention just to get it onto a ship.

It was a flawless plan.

Unfortunately, nobody had consulted the tide.

When we arrived to board the ferry the tide was low, the angle of the loading ramp presented absolutely no problem whatsoever and we simply drove straight on like everybody else.

No collection of concerned loading staff. No careful hand signals. No admiring passengers wondering whether they were looking at some exotic piece of Japanese machinery.

We just drove on.

Something doesn't feel right

At some point I also replaced the worn-out shock absorbers. Once again I used second-hand parts — there wasn't much point spending a fortune on a car that had originally cost me nothing.

With the replacement suspension fitted, Liam and I went out for a test drive.

After about ten miles I began to think I'd made things worse. The car didn't feel right at all. In fact, for a car with its newly improved suspension, it felt decidedly less reassuring than it had before.

I pulled over, got out and went around the car looking for the problem.

I'd forgotten to tighten the wheel nuts.

Apparently my mechanical quality-control procedure at the time consisted of driving ten miles and seeing what fell off.

Fortunately nothing did.

The final journey

Eventually the 300ZX's luck ran out.

We were on our way to visit my parents, who were staying in their caravan at Ledbury, when the engine finally gave up.

There wasn't going to be another collection of second-hand parts and another resurrection this time. The kind man from the RAC eventually arrived, loaded us up and transported us home.

That was effectively the end of my ownership of the 300ZX.

I put it on eBay and sold it for £300.

Financially, therefore, it was possibly one of the most successful cars I've ever owned. I bought it for nothing, drove it around for a while, took it to Guernsey, nearly lost a wheel, blew the engine up and then sold it for £300.

I'm not sure that's quite how classic-car investment is supposed to work, but I'll take it.

Still moving.
