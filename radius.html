Radius

CBSL, Radius CBSL, Radius Professional, Radius or Practice Net – the company went through quite a few name changes over the years. I started with CBSL on March 1st 1987.

CBSL, Radius CBSL, Radius Professional and eventually Practice Net — the company went through quite a few names during the years I worked there. Whatever name happened to be above the door, I usually just called it CBSL. It was easier that way.

I started with CBSL on 1 March 1987, although technically I wasn't their first choice. I'd left the Army in September 1986 and, after about three months working at a BP petrol station to supplement my dole money, applied for a job with CBSL.

They gave it to somebody else.

Fortunately for me, he didn't work out and a couple of weeks later they contacted their second choice.

Me.

I must have done something right because, one way or another, they kept me around for the next 27 years.

Before my time

CBSL stood for Computerised Business Systems Limited and had been around since the 1960s. Its home when I joined was Wroughton Place in Ely, Cardiff.

The business had started in an age when owning a computer was beyond the reach of most ordinary companies. Instead, businesses sent their information to computer bureaux such as CBSL, which processed things such as payroll and accounts on their behalf.

I've since discovered that CBSL's history went back much further than I appreciated when I worked there. In 1974 the company was offering a payroll and costing service called Paypack, running on its Burroughs B3500 mainframe. It already had customers across the UK and could process several clients together from magnetic tape.

None of that meant much to me when I walked through the door in 1987.

There was still a room full of women inputting customers' payroll information and the remains of that older world of computing were all around us. The most impressive was the enormous Burroughs B3500.

I eventually watched a scrap dealer attach a winch to this once hugely expensive piece of technology, drag it out of the building and cart it away. I assume that by then its greatest value was the metal and precious metals contained inside it.

Times were changing. Computers were becoming small and affordable enough for businesses to have their own, and instead of bringing their work to CBSL, customers increasingly wanted CBSL's software running in their own offices.

That's where I came in.

Fifteen wires and a soldering iron

CBSL wanted someone with electrical experience who could install clean power supplies and the data cabling required for these new-fangled computers. The clean-power side of the job didn't last particularly long, although I installed quite a few of them.

The cabling certainly did.

The early customer systems I worked on were generally rack-mounted computers running Xenix and later SCO Unix, with dumb terminals connected to them by serial cable.

We used 15-core cable which CBSL had specially manufactured for us by a company in Swansea. Even the outer sheath was printed with the CBSL name and telephone number:

0222 837410

It arrived on enormous one-kilometre drums and I would rewind it onto more manageable drums of about 300 metres before taking it out to customers. The permanent cable ran from the computer room to wall boxes around the building, with short "fly leads" connecting the equipment at either end.

And every connection had to be soldered.

I must have soldered literally millions of individual wires into 25-pin plugs and sockets over the years. I always maintained that there was no need to test my soldering because I never made a mistake.

I'm sticking with that version of events.

We later moved to a smaller cable and, even after all these years, I can still remember the sequence:

Screen, Green, Blue, Brown, Red, Orange, Yellow.

Not eight wires, I know. Only six plus the screen were actually used.

There were other technologies along the way — coaxial cable, Token Ring and various other relatively short-lived solutions — before we eventually arrived at UTP, Unshielded Twisted Pair.

UTP made life considerably easier. No more sitting there soldering plugs: punch the wires down in the correct order and the job was done. There was another bonus too. By then it was cheaper to buy ready-made fly leads than pay me to make them.

Progress!

The engineering department

During my early CBSL days there were really only two of us regularly travelling around the country doing the engineering work: myself and my immediate boss, Brian Phipps.

Above Brian was Adrian Pike, then Brian Parry, our Engineering Director, followed by Dale Rogers and Ian Monro.

We didn't mingle very much with the programmers. They were the brains of the company, hidden away producing the software while we were the people who went out and made it work in the real world. There also seemed to be quite a turnover of programmers, so I remember far more names from the engineering side.

Brian Parry is someone I remember particularly fondly. He was one of the nicest people you could ever meet. By the late 1990s he was desperate to retire but hadn't yet reached 65, so he hung on hoping redundancy might eventually give him the opportunity to leave early.

Sadly it never happened. Brian developed cancer and died before he got the chance to enjoy the retirement he'd been looking forward to.

Funny how memories work. When I first started writing about him I couldn't remember his surname. By the time I'd finished recalling what a lovely man he was, Parry had come back to me.

PCs arrive

At first, the arrival of PCs didn't actually change our systems very much. Instead of a dumb terminal on the user's desk there might now be a PC, but it would still connect through its serial port to the Unix computer and run terminal-emulation software.

The expensive new PC was essentially pretending to be the dumb terminal it had replaced.

Gradually that changed too, and my job changed with it.

My education: one discarded ICL

Some of the most useful computer training I ever had happened at home and started with a machine somebody else had thrown away.

My father worked as a handyman for Welsh Water during the 1990s and into the early 2000s. One day he came home with an old discarded ICL 286-class PC, complete with a 14-inch monitor and keyboard, and gave it to me.

It was magnificent.

It had DOS installed on an enormous 80MB hard drive, a massive 2MB of memory and VGA graphics.

Because it was mine and hadn't cost me a fortune, I could experiment.

So I did.

I took it apart, put it back together, added things, removed things and generally fiddled with everything I could find. This was before Plug and Play took the fun out of it, when installing another card could mean changing IRQs and I/O addresses until all the bits of your computer finally agreed to talk to each other.

Eventually I upgraded it from 2MB to a truly unbelievable 8MB of RAM. The memory cost me £80, bought half-price at the back door of the Radius CBSL office. At the time, 8MB seemed extravagant.

I installed Windows 3.1.

I fitted a network card despite not actually having a home network to connect it to. I didn't particularly need one; I just wanted to know how it worked.

Then came modems. I started with a 2400-baud modem and eventually worked my way up to the dizzying speed of 19,200 baud.

That old ICL probably taught me more about PCs than any formal training course could have done. I wasn't following instructions; I was taking things apart, trying things and figuring out why they either worked or didn't.

Life on the road

In the early days Brian Phipps and I travelled to customer sites in his sign-written CBSL Transit, later rebranded Radius CBSL. I was still in my twenties and thought of Brian as the "old" Brian because he was probably somewhere in his early fifties.

Early fifties!

Funny how your definition of old changes.

Brian later employed another engineer, Chris Bailey, who was given a Bedford Astramax. Chris and I then travelled together while Brian increasingly stayed back at the office.

Chris and I had great fun. We were young, away from home and living in hotels for much of the week. Eventually Chris left CBSL and I inherited the Astramax.

By the 1990s I could probably have pointed out two or three CBSL customers in most towns and cities across England and Wales. There were accountants and solicitors, but also much larger organisations such as the Christie-Tyler furniture group.

Three or four nights away during a working week wasn't unusual. At one point I was doing roughly 4,500 business miles every month in that Astramax.

Conveniently, its warranty servicing interval was also 4,500 miles.

I therefore didn't really have to calculate when the next service was due. I simply booked it for roughly the same date the following month.

All those years of filling out mileage expense claims had another effect. Distances between towns became lodged permanently in my head. Even today I can usually make a surprisingly good guess at the road mileage from Bridgend to somewhere I've not visited for years.

Some journeys were ridiculous. One particularly memorable two-day run took me from Bridgend to King's Lynn, then Goole, then Irvine in Scotland and finally back to Bridgend.

It wasn't exactly a nine-to-five desk job.

The scrap-metal beer fund

As technology changed, there was always old equipment to dispose of. Around the time we moved out of Wroughton Place, my boss and I would load obsolete servers into our vans and take them to the scrapyard.

The van was weighed on the way in, emptied, then weighed again on the way out and we were paid for the difference.

We were allowed to keep the money.

As we spent so much time staying away in hotels, this became our unofficial beer fund.

Different times.

Then came Radius

This is one part of the story where my memory and the historical records don't line up perfectly.

I remember CBSL joining with Radius at around the beginning of the 1990s, and to those of us working there it was presented as a merger. Contemporary business records describe it as an acquisition and appear to put the formal transaction earlier than I remember.

Either way, CBSL became Radius CBSL, and that is how I remember the next chapter of the company.

Looking back at old industry material has shown me just how substantial CBSL was around this period. Its accountancy software had hundreds of installations and the company had built a customer base across the country from Cardiff. That certainly fits the world I remember travelling around.

We eventually moved out of Wroughton Place, probably around 1991, and subsequently occupied offices in Cathedral Road. Later there was a purpose-built office on Excelsior Road called Alexander House, which I remember as having been named after Ian Alexander Monro.

The wider Radius Group went through various mergers and acquisitions too. One involved IT Professional, based somewhere around the Pershore area if my memory serves me correctly.

I only visited their building once, when we were effectively asset-stripping the place. Those employees who wanted to remain with the organisation were being relocated to other Radius companies.

For me, however, the visit produced a significant technological windfall.

There were hard drives sitting on a shelf and I was told I could take one home. I looked through them and spotted one whose part number appeared to have a "1" followed by an impressive number of zeroes.

That'll do.

It turned out to be a one-gigabyte hard drive.

One whole gigabyte!

I was over the moon. I genuinely couldn't imagine how I would ever manage to fill it. I think this would have been around 1992 or 1993.

From cable guy to computer engineer

Around 1995 or 1996 I was transferred to another part of the group, Radius Computer Maintenance, based in Darlaston in the Midlands.

Initially I was their cable guy.

My new manager was Rick Palmer, and Rick deserves a lot of credit for what happened next. He kept pushing me to improve myself and broaden what I could do rather than allowing me to remain simply the bloke who installed the cables.

Gradually I became a computer maintenance engineer.

Between what I was learning at work and what I was doing to that poor ICL computer at home, my job was moving a long way from the one I'd originally joined CBSL to do.

Back to my roots

Meanwhile the old CBSL side of Radius had continued changing.

Ian Monro retired and Eric Mcfarlan became Managing Director. My own recollection is that the restructuring during that period was difficult for the company and contributed to what happened next.

Eventually there was a management buyout involving Dale Rogers, Rob Shaw and Jean Shaw.

Following the buyout they asked me to come back.

I did.

By then I'd become their general-purpose technical person — basically the "do anything" guy.

I installed cabling, repaired computers, built PCs and servers, installed Unix and Windows systems and eventually progressed to designing complete systems for customers.

I would work out what equipment a customer needed and then give the specification to a salesman to sell it.

There was a very good reason why I didn't discuss prices with customers.

Whatever I thought something would cost, I'd probably quote about 50 percent too little.

Technical specifications were therefore my department.

Money was somebody else's problem.

Radius Professional becomes Practice Net

The business eventually became Radius Professional and then, in 2001, Practice Net.

The customer base changed during the Radius years too. Radius took over the Christie-Tyler work and the solicitors, while the old CBSL side increasingly concentrated on accountants.

That's one reason I still tend to call it CBSL when talking about those years. "Radius" could mean several different companies and operations; CBSL identifies the particular strand of the business I mean.

Over the years the company became much smaller. Today Practice Net is a tiny operation compared with the company I originally joined, although I still occasionally get dragged back into things.

An unexpected phone call

Then one day I received a phone call from somebody I'd previously worked with. He was now at CGI and asked whether I fancied a job there.

I got rather excited.

After all these years somebody had finally head-hunted me!

Well...not quite.

It turned out CGI paid employees a couple of hundred pounds for successfully introducing somebody. He'd apparently tried everybody else he knew and had finally got down to me.

Still, a job's a job.

More importantly, the office was in Bridgend.

After decades of driving all over Britain and spending nights in hotels, the prospect of going to work locally was enormously attractive.

So in 2015 I left Practice Net and joined CGI.

One decision I'd change

If I have one major regret from my Radius years, it was not joining the company pension scheme.

At the time the contribution might have been £40 or so and, being younger, I could always think of something more immediately useful to spend £40 on.

Usually beer.

With hindsight, that wasn't one of my better financial decisions. Had I taken advantage of the pension, perhaps I'd be retired by now instead of still working 12-hour shifts at CGI.

But hindsight has a habit of being considerably wiser than the person making the original decision.

Except I never really left

Leaving Practice Net didn't completely end my connection with the company. Even now I still occasionally do work for them on a daily rate.

One of those customers is Rowland Hall.

In 2025 I was doing some cabling work there when I came across something rather special still attached to the wall: a surviving length of the old CBSL 15-core serial cable we'd been installing decades earlier.

I cut a short piece off and kept it.

Somewhere in my house today is a little piece of cable bearing the CBSL name and that old Cardiff telephone number, 0222 837410.

From soldering irons to servers

When I joined CBSL on 1 March 1987, part of my job involved sitting with a soldering iron and connecting individual wires to 25-pin plugs so that somebody could use a dumb terminal connected to a Unix computer.

Over the years I went from serial cable to coax, Token Ring and UTP; from dumb terminals to PCs; from DOS and Unix to Windows; from installing cables to repairing computers; and eventually to specifying, building and installing complete server and PC systems.

I've watched computers that once cost fortunes being dragged away for scrap. I've been ridiculously excited about owning a one-gigabyte hard drive. I've paid £80 for some memory and thought I'd got an absolute bargain.

I've installed a network card in a computer despite having no network.

And I've driven enough miles around Britain that, decades later, many of the distances are still stored somewhere in my head.

There are undoubtedly dates I've remembered incorrectly and names and stories that will come back to me later. Somewhere in the attic I still have old expense claims and a couple of years' work diaries which may settle a few arguments with my memory.

But perhaps my favourite surviving artefact is simply that piece of old cable.

Nearly forty years after CBSL gave the job to their second choice, I'm still occasionally looking after one of the businesses connected with that same company.

Apparently their second choice didn't work out too badly after all.

Still moving.
