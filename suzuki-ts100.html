Suzuki TS100 — My First Bike

The good-looking one in the middle is a 16-year-old me. Susan is on the left and her friend Kim is on the right.

Chepstow

On 15th September 1981, aged 16 years and not quite two months, I joined the Army as a Boy Soldier and began training as an Apprentice Electrician in the Royal Engineers.

For the next two-and-a-bit years I would be based at the Army Apprentices College in Chepstow — AAC to those of us who were there.

The first six months were taken up almost entirely with basic military training.

We were taught how to look after ourselves properly: washing, shaving, cleaning our teeth, showering, laundry and generally maintaining a level of personal hygiene that was presumably considered essential before allowing us anywhere near explosives or electrical equipment.

We had to shave every day even though most of us barely had enough facial hair to justify owning a razor.

There was also the small matter of keeping the accommodation block immaculate.

The linoleum floors had to be waxed and polished using a machine called a bumper, the communal bathrooms and toilets had to be cleaned, and our clothes and bedding had to be folded and stacked with military precision.

I never quite mastered that last part.

As a result, during morning inspections the contents of my locker — and sometimes my entire bed pack — would occasionally be launched out of the window by an unimpressed NCO.

Running Everywhere

We marched on the drill square and learned all the usual drill movements.

In fact, if we weren't marching in formation, we were probably running in formation.

Everywhere.

Some of the lads joined the college band, so occasionally we even had music to run and march to.

Nowadays some of the treatment would probably be labelled bullying, and there's no question that we were shouted at a lot and frequently ordered to do things that seemed completely pointless at the time.

But looking back, I understand the thinking behind much of it.

It was about discipline: learning to follow an instruction immediately and without debate, even when you couldn't see the reason for it.

One day that discipline might save your life.

Under Canvas

We went away on various training exercises where we'd sleep under canvas.

I use the word sleep rather generously.

Just as you began to drift off, somebody would prod you awake because it was your turn on guard duty, or suddenly there would be an urgent requirement to patrol somewhere in the middle of the night.

We swam across rivers, canoed in them, washed in them, dug trenches beside them and occasionally dug trenches around the tents in an attempt to stop rainwater washing us into them.

We fired SLR rifles on the ranges near Caldicot, slept under the stars and, naturally, ran everywhere.

Then there were the morale-boosting competitions, including the infamous log run.

For weeks we'd run around camp with six or eight of us carrying a huge log while one poor soul ran out in front pulling on a rope.

The haircuts in that photo are worth mentioning too.

New Romantic music was all the rage, and the unofficial rule seemed to be: what's under your beret is yours.

The assault course beneath the Severn Bridge was another regular form of entertainment.

I actually enjoyed it, although some of the walls were quite a challenge for my 5'6" frame.

Learning a Trade

After those first six months we moved on to the electrical apprenticeship proper.

That period is much more of a blur.

I remember marching down the hill in formation to the training sheds, where the instructors always seemed to wear brown workshop coats.

I remember drilling Rawlplug-sized holes using nothing more sophisticated than a hammer and drift. Power tools were apparently considered an unnecessary luxury.

We also learned all sorts of mathematical calculations, including how many wires could legally fit inside a length of conduit.

The answer, as far as my teenage brain was concerned, was usually remarkably similar to however many wires you could physically force into it.

Military training continued at weekends, and of course we still ran everywhere, but life became ever so slightly easier.

I still occasionally had the contents of my locker thrown out of the window, but not quite as often.

Best of all, we were eventually allowed off camp.

At first I travelled home on leave by train from Chepstow to Bridgend.

That was about to change.

My First Motorbike

I can't remember exactly who paid for it or even precisely when I got it, but I'm fairly sure Mam and Dad bought my first bike for me: a white Suzuki TS100.

I suspect the logic was that if I had my own transport, I'd be able to come home on leave more often.

I was still only 16 when I got it, so for a couple of months I couldn't legally take it onto the road.

Instead, whenever I was home, I rode it round and round the roughly one-third-acre garden at my parents' new house in Pen-y-Fai.

I've always told people that my parents moved house without telling me after I'd gone away to Chepstow.

That isn't actually true, but it makes a much better story.

Although the new house did only have two bedrooms.

Hmm.

The garden was completely overgrown with brambles when they moved in, so Dad recruited me and Frenchie — Mark Allen — to help clear them.

In my memory, Frenchie and I hacked heroically through acres of brambles until we'd transformed the wilderness into an oasis.

In reality, Dad probably did nearly all of the work while the pair of us helped for a day or two before getting bored and wandering off to find something more interesting.

Freedom at Seventeen

When my 17th birthday finally arrived, the L plates went onto the bike and I rode it all the way back to Chepstow.

Fifty-two miles.

On a 100cc Suzuki.

Using A-roads through Cardiff and Newport.

It seemed to take forever.

I loved every minute of it.

I was hooked on motorbikes from then on.

Hobbies — Whether We Wanted Them or Not

To stop us becoming bored or homesick, we were required to take up two evening hobbies.

One of mine was swimming, which suited me perfectly because I'd grown up about 100 metres, as the crow flies, from the pool in Llangeinor.

The other was cycling.

I was fairly good at that too, having spent most of my childhood riding everywhere on a pushbike.

Once the Suzuki arrived, though, cycling rapidly lost some of its appeal and my second hobby somehow became motorcycling instead.

The college brought in a local school of motoring to train those of us with bikes and prepare us for our tests.

My test was in Cwmbran.

In January.

In the snow.

Passing My Test

I passed my motorcycle test on 23rd January 1983, which immediately proves that at least one version of my remembered timeline is completely wrong.

I can actually remember the test surprisingly well: arriving at the test centre, the little car park outside and the building itself.

Funny how certain things remain crystal clear while other memories disappear completely.

This was long before examiners followed riders around in cars giving instructions over helmet intercoms.

The examiner simply stood at the roadside, stopped me every so often to tell me where he wanted me to go, then positioned himself at various points around the route to watch me ride past.

He even warned me in advance that the emergency stop would happen on the next circuit.

So all I really had to do was keep an eye out for him standing at the side of the road and hit the brakes when he raised his clipboard.

Easy.

Then it was back to the test centre for the result.

Remember, it had actually been snowing during part of the test.

🏍️ I PASSED! 🎉

The L plates came off immediately and went straight into the bin.

Then I made my first major post-test error.

I headed down the A4042 towards Newport and joined the M4.

What a mistake.

The little 100cc Suzuki could barely manage 50 mph while lorries thundered past, covering me in spray, muck and road sludge.

After that experience, I decided the M4 and the TS100 didn't really belong together.

The Chepstow Bikers

There was a small group of us on camp who had motorbikes.

We'd ride around fairly aimlessly, as teenagers do, and eventually end up at the New Inn.

I'd have a Coke — don't forget I was still only seventeen — we'd play a couple of games of pool, put some punk, new wave or New Romantic music on the jukebox and then head back to camp ready for another day of hard graft.

That photo was taken outside the old Admin Block.

Strangely, I don't remember the building at all.

Off Road With Dad

When I was home on leave, Dad and I sometimes went riding off-road up on the common.

He had a Suzuki TS185ER.

We'd supposedly be following the local hunt, which was at least a plausible excuse for being up there.

I hated the off-road riding.

I've always been blessed with fairly short legs — some might say very short legs — so if the bike ended up straddling a bank or sitting on top of a deep wheel rut, my feet simply couldn't reach the ground.

The inevitable result was that I'd fall over.

Oh yes.

I also fell off in a river.

The Ford

We'd had several days of heavy rain when I went out for a ride and ended up at Castle-upon-Alun.

Anyone who knows the area will know the ford where the river crosses the road.

Normally it wasn't much of an obstacle.

That day the river was flowing considerably faster than normal.

I looked at it and decided I could make it.

I couldn't.

I ended up sitting in the middle of the river with the Suzuki beside me.

The force of the water had simply taken the bike out from underneath me.

I walked to the nearest house and phoned Dad, who came to rescue both me and the bike in his Datsun pickup.

Back home, I removed the exhaust, tipped the water out, bolted it back into place and gave the kick-start a try.

She fired up first kick.

They built them properly back then.

Memories of Chepstow

Chepstow left me with far more than an electrical apprenticeship.

It gave me lifelong friends.

There was a little gang of us: Mo — Ian Moorhouse — Eddie Canning, Frank O'Meara, Ginge Brown, Vardy — Ian Vardy — Smiffy and several others whose names will undoubtedly come back to me eventually.

A few of us had our names tattooed onto our ankles.

Probably because that was somewhere our mothers wouldn't immediately see them.

Mine was done in the style of the Sex Pistols lettering.

Pay Parades

During our first six months we weren't given full access to our Army pay.

Instead, the paymaster issued us £15 each week for “essentials”.

For most of the lads, essentials meant cigarettes.

For me, it meant sweets.

A packet of twenty JPS or similar cost less than £1 in 1981.

Haircuts

I can't remember who officially cut our hair, but I think one of the staff members may have earned a few extra beer tokens doing it.

Mam still cut mine whenever I went home on leave.

One day the RSM pulled me aside and demanded to know what the f&%# had happened to my hair.

He took me somewhere quiet and “straightened” it for me.

I became rather more cautious about Mam's haircuts after that.

Leave

There was no leave during the first six months and very little contact with family.

I think part of the idea was to help everyone get through the homesickness stage and settle into military life.

Strangely, I never really felt homesick at all.

Exercise

I loved the physical training.

I was already reasonably fit compared with some of the others because I'd spent most of my childhood outside, cycling, swimming and wandering over mountains.

We'd go running through the lanes around Beachley and Tutshill in ranks of three.

If the lad in front began to slow down, it wasn't unusual to put a hand on his back and push him along until he'd recovered enough to continue.

Swimming

Swimming was one of my regular hobbies.

We'd go to the camp pool — apparently now covered over and used as a squadron bar — and swim for an hour or two under the supervision of one of the trade instructors.

Length after length after length.

The Triangle Games

The Triangle Games were swimming competitions between junior establishments from the Army, Royal Navy and Royal Air Force.

We'd travel to other camps, compete, and then make the journey back the same day.

I managed a couple of first places in backstroke and several seconds in the individual medley.

Bike Number Two

The TS100 had done its job perfectly.

It had given me independence, got me home on leave, taught me how to ride properly, carried me through my motorcycle test and even survived being drowned in a Welsh river.

But once I could afford something bigger, there was only ever going to be one outcome.

Bike number two was purchased.
