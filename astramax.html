AstraMax

Slow, overloaded and covering around 4,500 miles a month. For several years the AstraMax was less a company van and more my mobile workshop.

Bedford/Vauxhall AstraMax

There couldn't have been too many vans left that required you to change down a gear just to get up a motorway incline, but this was one of them.

It wasn't quick, it wasn't glamorous, but it was a good workhorse — which was probably just as well, because I spent an enormous amount of my working life in one.

How I inherited it

When I first started at CBSL I normally travelled to customer sites with my boss, Brian Phipps, in his sign-written CBSL Transit. There were only the two of us regularly going out doing the engineering work in those days.

That changed when Brian employed another engineer, Chris Bailey.

Chris was given a Bedford AstraMax as his company van and the two of us started travelling around the country together, leaving poor old Brian back in the office. I say "old" Brian — he was probably only in his late forties or maybe early fifties, but when you're in your twenties that seems ancient.

Chris and I had a great time. We were away for three or four nights most weeks, staying in hotels all over the country and, being young and away from home, generally enjoying ourselves rather more than perhaps we should have.

When Chris eventually left CBSL, I inherited his AstraMax.

It was the start of a very long relationship between me and company vehicles.

My mobile workshop

I eventually had two AstraMax company vans. The first was a Bedford AstraMax 1.6 diesel and the second a Vauxhall AstraMax 1.7 diesel. They were essentially the same vehicle; the change from Bedford to Vauxhall was mainly a marketing decision.

They were supplied and leased by the company for my work, and mine were sign-written, first with the CBSL name and later Radius CBSL, so there was never any hiding who I worked for.

I was fortunate that I was also allowed to use the van privately, although private mileage wasn't free. Every month I had to account for exactly how much of the mileage was business and how much was mine.

My expense claim listed the mileage for every journey to customers and hotels, together with the van's starting and finishing mileage for the month. I'd total all the business journeys and subtract them from the overall mileage. Whatever was left was my private mileage.

Then came the fuel receipts. I'd add up everything I'd spent on fuel during the month and work out the actual fuel cost per mile. If, for example, I'd driven 100 private miles and fuel had worked out at 3.5p per mile, £3.50 would be deducted from my expense claim.

3.5p per mile for fuel. Those were the days.

A surprisingly good knowledge of British geography

Doing those calculations month after month probably explains why road distances became so firmly lodged in my head. When you've repeatedly claimed the mileage from Bridgend to Manchester, King's Lynn or Plymouth, eventually you stop needing to look it up.

There was, however, another reason I became rather good at remembering distances around Britain.

I discovered that I could usually add about 10% to the genuine mileage without anybody questioning it. There was no Google Maps for the ladies in accounts to check and, as they didn't spend their lives travelling around the country like I did, nobody was likely to know whether Bridgend to King's Lynn was 244 miles or 270.

So if the real journey was 244 miles, my expense claim might somehow remember it as being nearer 270.

That little bit of creative geography also conveniently helped to absorb some of my private mileage.

The funny thing is that after years of doing this I apparently trained my brain to remember the expense-claim distance rather than the real one. Even now, if somebody asks me how far away somewhere in Britain is, my instinctive answer tends to be about 10% too high.

So these days I mentally deduct 10% from my first estimate.

It turns out my internal sat-nav still has the CBSL expenses system installed.

4,500 miles until the next service

The service interval on the AstraMax was only 4,500 miles.

That sounds ridiculously short today, but it didn't really matter because I was covering about that distance every month.

So rather than keeping track of when the next service was due, I could more or less collect the van from one service and book it straight back in for the same time the following month.

The leasing company must have loved me.

By the 1990s CBSL had customers scattered all over England and Wales and spending three or four nights away in a week was perfectly normal. Accountants, solicitors and companies such as Christie-Tyler could send me almost anywhere.

Some journeys were particularly memorable. One two-day trip took me from Bridgend to King's Lynn, then across to Goole, up to Irvine in Scotland and finally all the way back to Bridgend.

The AstraMax might not have been very enthusiastic about motorway hills, but it certainly saw plenty of motorways.

Everything but the kitchen sink

I was never one for emptying the van out at the end of the day. It was usually full to the brim with cable, tools and all the other equipment I needed for installations.

On the roof was one of those long pipes for carrying lengths of trunking, together with a couple of sets of aluminium ladders strapped to the roof rack.

Aerodynamics weren't really a priority.

Neither, I suspect, was fuel consumption.

Given the amount of equipment I carried around, having to change down a gear on a motorway incline probably wasn't entirely the AstraMax's fault.

Not fast, but unforgettable

I can't pretend the AstraMax was an exciting vehicle. With all my tools, cable and ladders aboard, motorway inclines sometimes involved dropping down a gear and waiting patiently for the summit to arrive.

But looking back, that's probably why I remember it so fondly.

It wasn't a vehicle I'd chosen because I'd always dreamed of owning an AstraMax. It was my company van: supplied for the job, loaded with everything I needed and driven to customers all over the country. But because I was also allowed to use it privately, it became part of my life outside work as well.

For several years it was my transport, my mobile workshop and, with the amount of travelling I did, almost a second home.

At around 4,500 miles a month, I probably spent considerably more time with the AstraMax than I did with most people.
